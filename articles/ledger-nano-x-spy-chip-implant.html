Ledger Nano X spy-chip implant steals recovery phrases pixel by pixel

A hardware wallet keeps your secret in a hardened secure element built to resist a lab-grade attacker. The new implant doesn't fight the secure element at all — it taps the screen bus, reads the recovery words as pixels scroll by, and radios them out on a SIM. Devices now in circulation are built to survive physical inspection, and a $86M heist traced to one reseller is the likely first harvest.

Cybersecurity AdsNews Desk Oct 14, 2026 · 9 min read

A hardware wallet earns its trust by keeping your seed phrase inside a secure element — a hardened microcontroller designed to resist an attacker who owns a physical lab. A new class of implant, documented by Tibane Labs and reverse-engineered at depth by Joe Grand's Grand Idea Studio, sidesteps that protection entirely: it is a second, tiny computer soldered onto a genuine Ledger Nano X, wired straight to the OLED's data lines.

The trick works because a wallet still has to show you the 24 words once, during setup. The implant doesn't attack the secure element; it just watches the pixels pass by, decodes the letters of each word, and stores them. A cellular modem with its own eSIM and antenna then sends the phrase to the attacker. The case, packaging and behaviour all look normal, and Ledger's 'genuine device' check passes — because the secure element really is genuine.

How the implant keeps its cover

  • Tap point: the SPI bus between the Nano X secure element and the OLED screen — the recovery words travel over three wires, unencrypted.
  • Brain: a Cortex-M MCU (early units unmarked; later units a marked STM32G431, then markings scraped off).
  • Radio: an LTE modem plus a tiny eSIM and a custom flex antenna — from a hand-wound coil in Gen 1 to a moulded 'NANO' antenna hidden in the case shell.
  • Cover: Gen 1 was obvious (loose wires, a shrunk battery); the newest specs are coated to match the board and built to survive prying the device open.

The attack has matured across at least three generations, from a hand-wired prototype that Grand dissected at hardwear.io USA 2026 to bulk-installable units 'keyed to the Ledger's parts' that drop into place. The method is constant — tap the screen, read the words, send them out — while the concealment improves each round. If a 24-word phrase was ever displayed on a Nano X of uncertain origin, the only fix is a new seed generated on a device you trust, and moving funds onto it.

The public flashpoint: investigators report that more than $86 million in crypto may have been stolen from hundreds of wallets linked to devices sold by a Southeast Asian reseller, and Ledger has asked that reseller to halt sales while advising buyers not to set up their devices. The dollar figure and the exact connection remain not independently confirmed, but a tampered-device supply-chain attack is the leading explanation.

What this means for you

If you keep value in a hardware wallet

Buy directly from the manufacturer or a vetted channel, physically inspect the device before first use, and be wary of 'too cheap' or resold units from unknown third-party sellers. If you set one up and are unsure of its provenance, treat your recovery phrase as leaked: generate a fresh seed on a device you trust and migrate your assets. A stolen 24-word phrase is game over — no amount of cold-storage hardening rescues it.

The broader lesson is a stress-test on a long-held assumption: hardware security assumed the physical device was trustworthy. An attacker who controls the supply chain can ship you a 'genuine' wallet that is secretly a camera on your own secret. Secure by design, but only against the threat model you actually ship.
