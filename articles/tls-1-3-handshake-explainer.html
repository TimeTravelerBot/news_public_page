The TLS 1.3 handshake: why secure connections got faster and more private

A plain-English explainer of how the modern TLS 1.3 handshake works: one round trip, optional zero-round-trip resumption, only strong AEAD cipher suites, and stronger protection of sensitive connection details.

Explainer By the ADS News technology desk Oct 14, 2026 · 7 min read

What is the TLS 1.3 handshake

TLS (Transport Layer Security) is the protocol that puts a padlock on your browser: it encrypts the conversation between you and a server and proves the server is who it claims to be. TLS 1.3, finalized in 2018 as RFC 8446, is the fourth major version and is now the default on modern browsers and servers. The headline change is the handshake—the few messages of exchange the two sides complete before any real data flows—and 1.3 made it noticeably faster and more private while keeping the same security guarantees.

The speed win first: under 1.3, most connections complete in one round trip (1-RTT) instead of the two round trips that TLS 1.2 needs. That matters most for latency-sensitive sites, streaming, and mobile connections on high-latency links, because there is less back-and-forth before the first byte of page content. Even better, a returning client can often skip the round trip entirely with 0-RTT resumption, reusing a previous session so the very first data can be sent immediately. The trade-off is that 0-RTT data is replayable, so applications must treat it specially.

Security in 1.3 is also tightened at the core. Forward secrecy is mandatory: every connection uses ephemeral key exchange (ECDHE with curves such as X25519, P-256, or P-384), so a future break of a long-term private key cannot reveal past traffic. The allowed crypto is limited to strong AEAD cipher suites—AES-128-GCM, AES-256-GCM, and ChaCha20-Poly1305—while the legacy options that made TLS 1.2 vulnerable, such as RC4, SHA-1-based signatures, block ciphers in insecure modes, and static RSA key exchange, were removed entirely. A smaller, modernized cipher list means fewer weak corners for attackers to poke.

TLS 1.3 also improves privacy on the wire. The handshake no longer broadcasts a full ciphersuite preference or a certificate signature algorithm the way 1.2 did, and it uses a simplified message set with built-in downgrade protection that resists a middlebox or attacker trying to coerce your connection back to an older protocol. Together these changes mean an observer on your path learns less about what you are doing and has fewer levers to degrade your security, even when you are not running a TLS 1.3-only server.

What this means for you

In practice

Most people experience TLS 1.3 as a faster, quieter load, not a visible feature: modern browsers and major CDNs have enabled 1.3 by default for years. If you run a server or app, confirm your stack negotiates TLS 1.3 and that a TLS 1.2 fallback remains for older clients, then disable the legacy ciphers 1.3 dropped. If you are debugging a slow secure connection, watch for whether it is actually completing in 1-RTT (or 0-RTT) rather than silently falling back to a slower older handshake.
